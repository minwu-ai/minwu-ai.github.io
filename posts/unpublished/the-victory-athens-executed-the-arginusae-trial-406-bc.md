## The Battle of Arginusae and the Trial That Ended Athens

In the summer of 406 BC, the Athenian fleet destroyed the Spartan navy off the [Arginusae Islands](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:abo:tlg,0032,001:1:6), east of Lesbos. Eight Athenian strategoi defeated the Spartan fleet under Callicratidas. With nearly three hundred ships engaged, it was the largest naval battle yet fought between Greeks — a crucial win for Athens. Losing would have meant total defeat, perhaps the slaughter and enslavement of their entire population.

The Spartans lost seventy ships and their admiral. They offered peace. Athens rejected it. Within weeks, the city had killed the men who won.

---

**What happened at sea was bad enough.** Due to incompetent subordinate leadership, sailor exhaustion, and a sudden storm, the commanders failed to rescue the crews of twenty-five disabled Athenian ships. Thousands of men, many of them injured, were left clinging to wreckage. The accusation that followed was that the generals abandoned the shipwrecked — and, crucially, failed to recover the bodies of the fallen.

That last point mattered enormously. The unburied dead of Arginusae were not simply a logistics failure. They were, in Athenian belief, souls trapped without passage.

Into this atmosphere of grief, the politician Callixenus moved. He introduced a motion for the assembly to vote on the generals' collective guilt, instituting an unconstitutional procedure that evaded the individual trials required under Athenian law for capital offenses. His proposal appealed directly to public outrage rather than evidentiary deliberation.

The legal defence was immediate. Euryptolemus brought a *graphe paranomon* against Callixenus, claiming the proposal was unlawful, but was forced to drop it under public pressure. A demagogue named Lyciscus made the threat explicit: he moved that anyone attempting to prevent the people from doing what they chose should share the generals' ballot. Opposition collapsed.

All of this happened in the presence of one man who did not collapse. In 406, Socrates was a member of the senate, and on the first day of the trial — serving as president of the *prytanis* — he resisted, first with colleagues and then alone, the illegal proposal of Callixenus. In Plato's *Apology* and Xenophon's *Hellenica*, Socrates is presented as the only *prytanis* who opposed the crowd's demand for a single collective vote.

It did not matter. Euryptolemus managed briefly to pass a motion requiring separate trials, but it was procedurally overturned. The generals were condemned as one. Only six had willingly returned to Athens — Pericles, Diomedon, Lysias, Aristocrates, Thrasyllus, and Erasinides. Two fled into exile; the six who remained were executed.

Among the dead: Pericles the Younger, son of the great statesman.

Not much later, the Athenians had a change of heart. Callixenus fled Athens when opinion turned against him, eventually returned, and died in poverty. Remorse, however, could not undo what had been done. The trial had sent a chill through the high command. The best Athenian generals were dead or in voluntary exile. In 405 BC, at Aegospotami, Lysander destroyed the Athenian navy. This effectively ended the war — Athens could neither import grain nor communicate with its empire without control of the sea.

---

**The historical record is clear on the sequence of events.** The interpretive debate runs deeper. The trial presents significant difficulties, due partly to the complexity of events and partly to their contradictory representation in our two surviving narrative sources, Xenophon (*Hellenica* 1.7) and Diodorus Siculus (13.101–103). Scholars disagree on whether the assembly was manipulated or genuinely expressing deeply held religious conviction — and that disagreement is itself instructive. The grief was real. The unburied dead were real. A faction simply knew how to work with the material.

Debra Hamel's [*The Battle of Arginusae*](https://press.jhu.edu/books/title/11037/battle-arginusae) (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2015) remains the most accessible modern synthesis of battle and trial. She identifies a structural irony running through the whole Athenian command system: in order to tolerate the aggregation of military expertise in a regime predicated on institutionalised amateurism, the Athenians subjected their commanders to extreme democratic accountability — accountability that, under sufficient pressure, became indistinguishable from mob justice.

The pattern is not obscure. A society under existential strain, a genuine failure by leaders, grief expertly weaponised, procedural protections dismantled by a single threat, and one dissenter who could not stop it. Athens did not lose the Peloponnesian War at Arginusae. It lost the war when it decided that winning was less important than punishing the winners.
