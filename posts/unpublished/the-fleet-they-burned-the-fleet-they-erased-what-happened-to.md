In 1477, a eunuch official at the Ming court asked the Ministry of War for the logs of the great treasure fleets — the sailing charts, the crew rosters, the records of seven voyages that had carried Chinese ships as far as East Africa. The files were gone. The vice-president of the ministry at the time, a scholar-official named Liu Daxia, is widely credited — in accounts written down roughly a century later — with having hidden or burned them himself, reportedly telling a superior that the old voyages had wasted money and lives and that the records should disappear so the idea could never be proposed again.

That story comes to us secondhand, from the Ming-dynasty writer Yan Congjian, and historians treat it carefully rather than as courtroom fact. But the underlying event — that the archive was gone by the late 1470s, at exactly the moment someone in government wanted to consult it — is not seriously disputed. Something happened to those records, and it happened inside the bureaucracy, not on some foreign shore.

**The voyages, briefly**

Between 1405 and 1433, the Ming court sent the eunuch admiral [Zheng He](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Zheng-He) on seven expeditions with fleets that dwarfed anything Europe would put to sea for another century — hundreds of ships on the largest voyages, crews numbering in the tens of thousands, reaching the Arabian Peninsula and the coast of East Africa. The expeditions projected Ming power, collected tribute, and brought back giraffes and envoys as proof of the emperor's reach.

They were also eunuch-run, eunuch-funded, and eunuch-glorifying — which made them a direct affront to the Confucian scholar-officials who staffed the civil bureaucracy and regarded the eunuch establishment as a corrupting rival power at court. The voyages depended almost entirely on imperial patrons who valued prestige projection: the Yongle Emperor, who initiated them, and later the Xuande Emperor, under whom Zheng He sailed his seventh and final voyage. He died on the return leg in 1433, probably off the coast of India or during the homeward passage — [accounts place his death in 1433](https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/snapshot/zheng-he-admiral-chinas-treasure-fleet), with Calicut as the likely location.

**What the bureaucrats did next**

After 1433, the voyages simply stopped. That part is often told as a story about Ming insularity — a civilization turning inward. But the more interesting and better-documented part is what the scholar-officials did to make the reversal permanent. Shipyards were allowed to decay, oceangoing ship construction was restricted by later edict, and — per the account centered on Liu Daxia — the paper trail that would have let a future emperor or admiral reconstruct the program was targeted directly.

This was not cost-cutting. The Confucian bureaucracy had lost the argument for decades while Yongle and Xuande backed the fleets; once the patrons who shielded the program were gone — Yongle in 1424, Zheng He himself in 1433 — the officials who had always opposed the voyages moved not merely to end funding but to make certain the underlying capability and justification could not be revived by a future ruler with similar ambitions. A policy without a surviving record is far harder to restart than a policy that is merely unfunded.

**The pattern, not the policy**

| Phase | What happened |
|---|---|
| Patron alive (1405–1433) | Yongle, then Xuande, shield the fleet; eunuch faction controls the program |
| Patron dies (1433) | Zheng He dies on the seventh voyage; no successor champion emerges |
| Faction turns (1430s–1470s) | Shipbuilding restricted; expertise lapses |
| Records sought (1477) | War Ministry archive reportedly missing or destroyed |

What this episode explains, fifteenth century or not, is how fragile a program becomes when its legitimacy rests on one person's standing rather than durable institutional buy-in. The treasure fleets were extraordinary, but they were never owned by the state the way, say, the civil examination system was owned by the state. They were owned by an emperor and his admiral. When both left the stage, the program had no constituency left to defend it — only a faction with every incentive to make sure it stayed dead.

And that faction did not stop at policy. It went after the evidence. This is the detail worth sitting with: destroying a budget stops a program for a cycle; destroying the record of how it worked, what it cost, and what it achieved stops the next generation from even knowing it's an option. Liu Daxia's reported justification — that hiding the old accounts served the realm by foreclosing the debate entirely — is a strikingly direct statement of a tactic that long outlives the Ming court.

**Where the parallel holds, and where it strains**
