In 1817, Albany banks lent New York $200,000 to help build a canal that had yet to carry a single boat. The state could tell the banks what it hoped to collect in tolls. What it could offer that day was its own credit. Why would anyone lend on a promise that big?

## 📜 A Federal Veto, a State Gamble

On March 3, 1817, his last full day in office, President James Madison [vetoed a bill](https://cf-test.millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/march-3-1817-veto-message-internal-improvements-bill) that would have set aside federal funds for roads, canals, and improvements to navigation. His reasoning was specific: he argued that the Constitution did not authorize the proposed federal role. He favored internal improvements, but believed a constitutional amendment was needed to support such a national program. He simply couldn’t find the authority for it in the document he’d helped write.

For New York, the veto closed off another prospect of federal support for a project it had spent years pursuing. A canal connecting the Hudson River to Lake Erie—363 miles across upstate New York—had been under official study since 1810. [DeWitt Clinton](https://www.canals.ny.gov/About/History), a former New York City mayor who would become governor in 1817, emerged as its most famous advocate. The undertaking would cost about $7 million, a staggering sum for the era, and critics derided it as “Clinton’s Folly” and “Clinton’s Ditch.”

New York had a plan for paying off the canal: collect tolls from the boats that used it. But first it had to pay people to dig it. The lenders had a question of their own: who would stand behind the debt if those tolls came up short?

## 🏦 Borrowing Against New York’s Credit

New York decided to proceed on its own credit. On April 15, 1817, the legislature authorized construction and established a dedicated Canal Fund. The [financing law](https://classic-literature.net/archer-butler-hulbert-1873-1933/the-great-american-canals-volume-2-the-erie-canal/) empowered its commissioners to borrow “on the credit of the people of this state.” Initially, annual borrowing and the fund’s net income together could not exceed $400,000; subsequent legislation authorized further loans as construction advanced. The project was financed over time, rather than through a single $7 million bond sale.

The distinction matters. The state expected canal earnings to help repay its borrowing, but investors were lending against New York’s credit. A dedicated repayment fund did not make the debt a promise secured exclusively by future tolls. Indeed, the legislation [had been amended](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/Whitford/1906/Chap02.html) to permit borrowing on the credit of the state rather than on the credit of the Canal Fund.

## 🧂 Salt, Taxes, and Future Tolls

The fund drew on several sources: canal tolls, [salt duties](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/Whitford/1906/Chap02.html), auction duties, a steamboat passenger tax, and grants and donations. The law also provided for a tax on lands within 25 miles of either side of the canals, although that tax was never collected. These arrangements gave the undertaking an identifiable repayment plan and separate accounts. They also show that taxes were part of the financing from the beginning.

Albany banks supplied the [initial $200,000 loan](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/merchmag/merchmag_v24_185101.pdf) in 1817.

Think of someone who takes out a loan in their own name to open a shop. They expect sales to cover the payments, but the bank has to decide whether to lend before the first customer walks in. The owner’s record of paying back what they’ve borrowed gives the bank a reason to trust them. If sales fall short, the owner still owes the money. For the canal, tolls were the hoped-for sales. New York was the borrower.

## 🚢 The Ditch Opens—and Pays Off

[Construction began at Rome, New York, on July 4, 1817](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/NYS1961/chap1.html). On October 26, 1825, Governor Clinton departed Buffalo aboard the *Seneca Chief* for the inaugural journey along the completed canal. The celebrations culminated in New York on November 4, when he poured Lake Erie water into the Atlantic in the symbolic [“Wedding of the Waters.”](https://www2.census.gov/prod2/decennial/documents/1880a_v18-08.pdf)

The wager paid off. Toll earnings helped cover the annual payments. Paying back the principal took longer. By June 1835, the Canal Fund commissioners [announced that funds were available](https://vanburenpapers.org/document-mvb06858) to redeem Erie and Champlain Canal stock due in 1837, offering premiums to holders who surrendered it early. The notice specified that interest on that stock would cease on July 1, 1837.

The canal’s commercial success also created pressure to expand it. By 1835, the legislature had [approved enlargement of the original channel](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/NYS1961/chap1.html). New York had built a transportation link whose growing traffic soon demanded more capacity.

## ⚖️ Who Actually Bore the Risk?

With a [general obligation bond](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products-0), lenders rely on the issuer’s broader credit, including its taxing power. With a revenue bond, they rely on specified revenues, which can include tolls, lease payments, or [dedicated taxes](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/municipal-bonds). At Erie, tolls were part of the repayment plan. New York’s own credit stood behind the borrowing.

## 🤝 How Trust Is Earned

When the Albany banks made that first loan, the tolls were still a forecast. By 1835, New York could offer to repay some of its canal debt ahead of schedule. Lenders could now see the canal working and the state setting aside money to repay them.

That’s what makes trust slow to build. People decide whether to believe your next promise by watching what you do with the ones you’ve already made. Break an important promise, and the next one carries less weight, however good the plan sounds. You can give people new reasons to trust you by following through again and again. It can take years, and some people may never trust you the same way again.
