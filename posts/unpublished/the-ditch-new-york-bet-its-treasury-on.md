On March 3, 1817, his last full day in office, President James Madison vetoed a bill that would have set aside federal funds for roads, canals, and improvements to navigation. His reasoning was specific: he argued that the Constitution did not authorize the proposed federal role. He favored internal improvements, but believed a constitutional amendment was needed to support such a national program. He simply couldn’t find the authority for it in the document he’d helped write. ([Madison’s veto message](https://cf-test.millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/march-3-1817-veto-message-internal-improvements-bill))

For New York, the veto closed off another prospect of federal support for a project it had spent years pursuing. A canal connecting the Hudson River to Lake Erie—363 miles across upstate New York—had been under official study since 1810. DeWitt Clinton, a former New York City mayor who would become governor in 1817, emerged as its most famous advocate. The undertaking would cost about $7 million, a staggering sum for the era, and critics derided it as “Clinton’s Folly” and “Clinton’s Ditch.” ([NYS Canals history](https://www.canals.ny.gov/About/History))

New York decided to proceed on its own credit. On April 15, 1817, the legislature authorized construction and established a dedicated Canal Fund. The financing law empowered its commissioners to borrow “on the credit of the people of this state.” Initially, annual borrowing and the fund’s net income together could not exceed $400,000; subsequent legislation authorized further loans as construction advanced. The project was financed over time, rather than through a single $7 million bond sale. ([1817 act, Appendix B](https://classic-literature.net/archer-butler-hulbert-1873-1933/the-great-american-canals-volume-2-the-erie-canal/))

The distinction matters. The state expected canal earnings to help repay its borrowing, but investors were lending against New York’s credit. A dedicated repayment fund did not make the debt a promise secured exclusively by future tolls. Indeed, the legislation had been amended to permit borrowing on the credit of the state rather than on the credit of the Canal Fund. ([Whitford’s canal history](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/Whitford/1906/Chap02.html))

The fund drew on several sources: canal tolls, salt duties, auction duties, a steamboat passenger tax, and grants and donations. The law also provided for a tax on lands within 25 miles of either side of the canals, although that tax was never collected. These arrangements gave the undertaking an identifiable repayment plan and separate accounts. They also show that taxes were part of the financing from the beginning. ([Whitford’s canal history](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/Whitford/1906/Chap02.html))

Private investors participated in that plan. Albany banks supplied the initial $200,000 loan in 1817. The state’s role was to organize construction and put its credit behind the borrowing, allowing private money to finance a public undertaking. ([Contemporary financial account](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/merchmag/merchmag_v24_185101.pdf))

Construction began at Rome, New York, on July 4, 1817. On October 26, 1825, Governor Clinton departed Buffalo aboard the *Seneca Chief* for the inaugural journey along the completed canal. The celebrations culminated in New York on November 4, when he poured Lake Erie water into the Atlantic in the symbolic “Wedding of the Waters.” ([New York legislative history](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/NYS1961/chap1.html), [Census historical account](https://www2.census.gov/prod2/decennial/documents/1880a_v18-08.pdf))

The wager paid off. Toll earnings helped meet the debt’s annual payments; repayment of the principal took longer. By June 1835, the Canal Fund commissioners announced that funds were available to redeem Erie and Champlain Canal stock due in 1837, offering premiums to holders who surrendered it early. The notice specified that interest on that stock would cease on July 1, 1837. That is a more precise account of the financial success than saying the canal repaid its entire construction debt in its first year. ([1835 redemption notice](https://vanburenpapers.org/document-mvb06858))

The canal’s commercial success also created pressure to expand it. By 1835, the legislature had approved enlargement of the original channel. New York had built a transportation link whose growing traffic soon demanded more capacity. ([New York legislative history](https://www.eriecanal.org/texts/NYS1961/chap1.html))

## What the structure actually did

Erie’s financing story turns on a distinction that the word “bond” can obscure: the source expected to make the payments and the security promised to investors are related, but they are not always identical.

A general obligation bond is backed by the issuer’s full faith and credit, including its taxing power. A revenue bond is backed by specified revenues, such as highway tolls or lease payments, rather than the government’s general taxing power. The pledged revenues can also include dedicated taxes. To understand who bears the risk, you have to examine the particular promise behind the bond. ([SEC explanation](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products-0), [NAIC explanation](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/municipal-bonds))

Revenue bonds now commonly finance transportation, water and sewer systems, hospitals, housing, and other facilities. When repayment depends on a project’s earnings, demand forecasts matter: fewer vehicles paying tolls or fewer customers paying fees can leave less money available for bondholders. But the consequences of a shortfall depend on the pledged revenues and the financing terms. A dedicated income stream alone does not tell you that the public treasury is insulated. ([NAIC explanation](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/municipal-bonds))

That is the lesson to carry back to Erie. New York combined public credit with a dedicated fund, earmarked taxes, and anticipated tolls to finance a project too large to pay for all at once. The tolls eventually rewarded the gamble. Until they did, the state’s credit helped turn an uncertain commercial future into money that could pay workers and contractors in the present.

The financial achievement was making that future credible enough to borrow against—and giving the borrowing a repayment plan that worked.
