In June 1941, the British gave a name to something they had decided to be afraid of. Not the German army, not the Luftwaffe — their own good fortune. The codeword was Ultra, and the name arose because the intelligence obtained was considered more important than the highest British security classification then used. The fear wasn't that they'd lose the secret to a spy. It was that they'd give it away themselves, by being too useful with it.

By then Bletchley Park's codebreakers — Alan Turing chief among them — had broken into the German military's Enigma cipher, building on earlier Polish work, and were reading messages that ranged from Hitler's own orders down to U-boat refueling schedules. [Britannica's account](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ultra-Allied-intelligence-project) describes how the intelligence gathered, code-named Ultra, provided the Allies with critical information, including Hitler's orders and battlefield reports, often within hours of transmission. This was not a one-time trick. It was a running advantage, renewed daily, for most of the war.

And it was almost unimaginably fragile. The Germans believed Enigma was unbreakable because, mathematically, it very nearly was — seventeen thousand-odd settings per message, changed daily. The one way to break that confidence wasn't a captured machine or a defector. It was pattern. If Royal Navy ships started reaching U-boat rendezvous points a little too often, if RAF fighters were suspiciously ready for raids that hadn't been announced, someone in Berlin would eventually ask the obvious question. The intelligence was perfect and perishable in the same instant it was used.

So the British built an entire apparatus whose purpose was to make sure almost nobody acted on Ultra directly. They invented a fictional master spy. [As Wikipedia's sourced account describes it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_(cryptography)), the code name "Boniface" was used as a cover name for Ultra — British intelligence created a fictional MI6 master spy who controlled a fictional series of agents throughout Germany, and information obtained through code-breaking was often attributed to the human intelligence from the Boniface network. Winston Churchill, by the historian Harry Hinsley's account, liked the fiction so well that [references to the invented agent](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v11/n14/f.h.-hinsley/what-did-they-do-with-it) kept turning up in his own wartime correspondence — references to the agent Boniface cropped up in his telegrams and letters till the end of the war.

Distribution was its own discipline. Special Liaison Units carried Ultra to field commanders under rules that forbade commanders from ever acting on it in a way that would reveal its source — an attack might need a reconnaissance plane sent over a target first, so that the "discovery" had a plausible, visible origin. A scholarly comparison of Bletchley's wartime practice to modern cybersecurity notes that the British exercised security discipline and operational restraint to keep Germany in the dark, and Ultra was often sanitized so that it might appear to come from some other source, for example, a human spy or aerial reconnaissance. Bletchley even used Ultra defensively on itself: BP actively targeted German intelligence agencies, continually searching for evidence of Ultra's compromise, and British counterintelligence also relied on Ultra to vet the reliability of double agents.

The most famous test of this discipline is also the most contested — which is worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. For decades, a story circulated that Churchill knew in advance that Coventry would be bombed on the night of 14 November 1940, and chose to let the city burn rather than risk tipping off the Germans that Enigma was broken. The claim originated with [F.W. Winterbotham's 1974 memoir](https://winstonchurchill.org/resources/myths/coventry-what-really-happened/) *The Ultra Secret*, the book that first revealed Ultra's existence to the public. But the historical record doesn't support it. Martin Gilbert's research for the Churchill archives found no such decrypt naming Coventry specifically, and Churchill's own private secretary at the time said Churchill received word that a massive bombing raid would begin that day, but assumed the target was London. A detailed account of the Ultra traffic that week concludes bluntly: Churchill could not have taken action on new Ultra intelligence on the afternoon or evening of the attack because there was none to provide him — there was a pause in Ultra decrypts from 01:15 GMT on November 13 to 02:40 GMT on November 15. The myth endures — it was even dramatized on stage — precisely because it captures something true about the *logic* of Ultra, even though it misdescribes this particular night.
